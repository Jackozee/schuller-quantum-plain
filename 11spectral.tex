Recall that the resolvent map \(R_A\colon\rho(A)\to\Cal L(H)\) sends
an element \(z\in\rho(A)\) to the bounded operator
\begin{equation}
  R_A(z) = (A-z)^{'} \colon \Cal H\to\Cal H
\end{equation}
where \((A-z)'\) is the composition
\begin{equation}
  (A-z)' = i_{\Cal D_A}\circ(A-z)^{-1}
,\end{equation}
of the inverse map \((A-z)^{-1}\colon\Cal H\to\Cal D_A\) and the
inclusion \(i_{\Cal D_A}\colon\Cal D_A\to\Cal H\).

Assume that \(A\) is self-adjoint. In this case we have
\(\rho(A)\subseteq\R\), so the resolvent map \(R_A\) is defined on
\(\H=\{z\in\C : \Im z > 0\}\).
Now, for every \(\psi\in\Cal H\), define the function
\(N_\psi\colon\H\to\C\) by
\begin{equation}
  N_\psi(z) = \langle \psi,R_A(z)\psi\rangle.
\end{equation}
One can prove that \(N_\psi\) is analytic on \(\H\). Moreover, it
satisfies
\begin{align}
  \Im N_\psi(z)
  &= \frac{
    \langle\psi,R_A(z)\psi\rangle
    -
    \langle R_A(z)\psi,\psi\rangle
  }{2i} \\
  &= \frac{
    \langle\psi,R_A(z)\psi\rangle
    -
    \langle\psi,R_A(\bar z)\psi\rangle
  }{2i} \\
  &= \frac{ \langle\psi,(R_A(z)-R_A(\bar z))\psi\rangle }{2i} \\
  &= \frac{ \langle\psi,(z-\bar z)R_A(z)R_A(\bar z)\psi\rangle }{2i} \\
  &= \langle\psi,\Im(z)R_A(z)R_A(\bar z)\psi\rangle \\
  &= \Im(z)\langle R_A(z)\psi,R_A(z)\psi\rangle \\
  &= \Im(z)\|R_A(z)\psi\|^{2}
,\end{align}
where we have used that \(R_A(z)^{*}=R_A(\bar z)\) and
the \emph{first resolvent formula}
\begin{equation}
  R_A(z)-R_A(w)=(z-w)R_A(z)R_A(w)
.\end{equation}
Since \(R_A(z)\) is injective, it follows that \(\Im N_\psi(z)>0\)
whenever \(\Im(z)>0\).
Such a function is called a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function.
Then, by a classical theorem
(see \cite[\S69, Thm 2]{akhiezer1961operators}),
the function \(N_\psi\) can be written in the form
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:nevanlinna}
  N_\psi(z)
  = \alpha + \beta z +
  \int_{\R}^{} 
  \left(
    \frac{1}{t - z}
    -
    \frac{t}{1 + t^{2}}
  \right)
    \d\mu_\psi(t)
\end{equation}
where the measure \(\mu_\psi\) is determined by \(N_\psi\).
Here, we won't derive the formula for \(\mu_\psi \) in full
generality.
Instead, we will see that the additional condition
\begin{equation}
  \sup_{y>0} |yN_\psi(iy)|<\infty.
\end{equation}
satisfied by our particular function \(N_\psi\),
will allow us to obtain the formula for \(\mu_\psi\) in an easier way.
Indeed, since this supremum is finite, we have an upper bound \(M\)
for the absolute values of the real and imaginary parts of \(yN_\psi(iy)\):
\begin{equation}
  \left\{
    \begin{aligned}
      \left| \alpha y + y \int_{\R}^{}
      \left(
        \frac{t}{t^{2}+y^{2}}-\frac{t}{1+t^{2}}
      \right) \d\mu_\psi(t)
      \right| &\leq M,
      \\
      \left| \beta y^{2}
        + y^{2}\int_{\R}^{} \frac{1}{t^{2}+y^{2}} \d\mu_\psi(t)
      \right| &\leq M,
    \end{aligned}
  \right.
\end{equation}
Dividing the first inequality by \(|y|\), the second by \(y^{2}\), and
taking \(y\to\infty\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \alpha = \int_{\R}^{} \frac{t\d\mu_\psi(t)}{1+t^{2}},
  \qquad
  \beta = 0,
\end{equation}
so the representation \eqref{eq:nevanlinna} takes the simpler form
\begin{equation}
  N_\psi(z)
  =
  \int_{\R}^{} \frac{\d\mu_\psi(t)}{t - z}.
\end{equation}
Knowing this, we can solve for \(\mu_\psi\) immediately,
using the following formula for the characteristic
function of the ray \((-\infty,t]\)
\begin{equation}
  \chi_{(-\infty,t]}(x)
  =
  \lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}
  \lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}
  \frac{1}{\pi}
  \int_{-\infty}^{t+\delta}\Im
  \Big(\frac{1}{x-(\tau+i\epsilon)}\Big)\d \tau.
\end{equation}
Indeed, integrating both sides against \(\mu_\psi(x)\), we obtain the
so-called Stieltjes inversion formula:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:inversion}
  \mu_\psi(-\infty,t]
  =
  \lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}
  \lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}
  \frac{1}{\pi}
  \int_{-\infty}^{t+\delta}\Im N_\psi(\tau+i\epsilon)\d \tau
.\end{equation}

Hence, in principle, we are capable of constructing the measures
\(\mu_\psi\) using only the function
\(N_\psi(z)=\langle\psi,R_A(z)\psi\rangle\).
By polarization, one constructs the complex measures
\(\mu_{\phi,\psi}\) and the unique projection-valued measure \(P\) satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \langle \phi, P(\Omega)\psi\rangle = \mu_{\phi,\psi}(\Omega).
\end{equation}
Finally, one proves that \(A=\int_{M}^{}\lambda\d P(\lambda)\).
